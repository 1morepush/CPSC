Asthma

About 3 min read

Key points

  • Asthma is a long-term condition that makes the airways in your lungs swell and narrow, so breathing gets harder.
  • Symptoms come and go. Flare-ups are often called asthma attacks.
  • There is no cure, but with the right plan most people keep asthma under control and live full, active lives.
  • A severe attack is an emergency. Call 911 if your quick-relief inhaler isn't helping.

What is asthma?

Your airways (bronchial tubes) carry air in and out of your lungs. With asthma, they are extra sensitive. When something bothers them, three things happen:

  • The muscles around the airways tighten.
  • The lining of the airways swells.
  • Extra mucus builds up.

Together, these make the airways narrower, so it is harder to move air in and out. Most attacks are short and get better with treatment. Sometimes an attack is so severe that it becomes life-threatening.

What are the symptoms?

  • Coughing, especially at night or early in the morning
  • Wheezing: a high-pitched whistling sound when you breathe out
  • Shortness of breath
  • Chest tightness, pain or pressure

Not everyone has every symptom, and your symptoms can change from one attack to the next.

What causes asthma, and who gets it?

Doctors don't know exactly why some people get asthma and others don't. These things raise the chances:

  • Having a parent with asthma
  • Having allergies
  • Smoking, or breathing other people's smoke
  • Air pollution, such as living in a busy city
  • Fumes and dust at work, for example in farming, hairdressing, painting, and plastics or electronics manufacturing
  • Lung infections in childhood
  • Low birth weight
  • Acid reflux (GERD)

Asthma can start at any age, but it often begins in childhood. In children it is more common in boys; after puberty it is more common in women.

What sets off an attack?

Things that set off asthma are called triggers. Common ones include pollen, dust, mold, pet hair and dander, smoke, colds and flu, cold air, exercise, strong smells, and stress. Your triggers may be different from someone else's, so it helps to notice what makes your symptoms worse.

How is asthma diagnosed?

Asthma can look like other conditions, such as COPD, early heart failure or vocal cord problems. To find out what is going on, your doctor will:

  • Ask about your symptoms and your health history
  • Examine you
  • Do breathing tests that measure how much air you can blow out and how fast. Often the test is repeated after you use an inhaler, to see whether your breathing improves.

How is asthma treated?

The goal is to prevent attacks and keep you feeling well. Treatment usually includes:

  • Avoiding your triggers as much as you can. This is one of the most helpful things you can do.
  • Controller medicine, taken every day even when you feel fine. It calms the swelling in your airways so attacks happen less often.
  • A quick-relief (rescue) inhaler, used during an attack. It opens the airways within minutes.
  • A peak flow meter, a small handheld device you blow into at home. It shows how well air is moving and can warn you that an attack is coming, sometimes before you feel it.
  • An asthma action plan, made with your doctor, that tells you what to take every day and what to do when symptoms get worse.

For some people with moderate to severe asthma, newer medicines given as a shot (called biologics) can help. Our clinic offers allergy testing and biologic treatment for patients who qualify.

When to get help right away

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if:

  • Your quick-relief inhaler isn't helping
  • You are too short of breath to talk or walk
  • Your lips or fingernails look blue or gray

A severe attack may need oxygen and medicine through an IV in the hospital.
