Lung Cancer

About 5 min read

Key points

  • Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in the United States, for both men and women.
  • Smoking causes about 85% of cases. Quitting at any age lowers your risk.
  • Early lung cancer often has no symptoms. If you smoke or used to smoke, ask whether a yearly screening scan is right for you.
  • Treatment depends on the type of lung cancer and how far it has spread.

What is lung cancer?

Lung cancer starts when cells in the lung begin to grow out of control. Over time they form a lump, called a tumor. If it isn't treated, cancer can spread to other parts of the body, such as the lymph nodes, bones, liver, adrenal glands or brain. This spread is called metastasis.

There are two main types, which grow and are treated differently.

Non-small cell lung cancer

This is the most common type, about 85% of lung cancers. It usually grows more slowly than small cell lung cancer. The main kinds are:

  • Adenocarcinoma, the most common lung cancer in the United States. It is linked to smoking, but it is also the most common type in people who have never smoked.
  • Squamous cell carcinoma, about 25% of lung cancers. It usually starts in the larger airways and is most often caused by smoking.
  • Large cell carcinoma, a less common type that doesn't fit the other two groups.

Small cell lung cancer

This type makes up about 15% of lung cancers and is almost always caused by smoking. The cancer cells are small but divide quickly, so it tends to grow and spread fast. Doctors describe it as limited (only in the chest) or extensive (spread beyond the chest). It usually responds to chemotherapy but is hard to cure.

What are the symptoms?

Lung cancer often causes no symptoms until it is advanced. When symptoms do appear, they may include:

  • A cough that doesn't go away or gets worse
  • Coughing up blood
  • Shortness of breath
  • Pain in the chest, shoulders or back
  • Weight loss or loss of appetite
  • Bronchitis or pneumonia that keeps coming back
  • Swelling of the face or arms

These symptoms often have other causes, but see a doctor if you have any of them, especially if you smoke or used to smoke.

What causes lung cancer?

  • Smoking cigarettes, pipes or cigars causes about 85% of lung cancers. Tobacco smoke contains thousands of chemicals, many of which are known to cause cancer.
  • Secondhand smoke from other people's tobacco.
  • Radon, a natural gas with no smell or color that can seep into homes from the ground. A simple home test can check for it.
  • Chemicals at work, such as asbestos, nickel and silica.
  • Family history of lung cancer.
  • Other lung diseases, such as COPD or pulmonary fibrosis.
  • A diet low in fruits, vegetables and grains may also raise the risk.

How common is lung cancer?

More than 200,000 Americans are diagnosed with lung cancer each year. About 1 in 16 people will get it during their lifetime. It is most common in people over 50 with a long history of smoking.

Screening: finding it early

Finding lung cancer early, before it spreads, gives the best chance of successful treatment. A yearly low-dose CT scan of the chest is recommended for adults who:

  • are 50 to 80 years old, and
  • have smoked at least 20 "pack-years" (for example, a pack a day for 20 years, or two packs a day for 10 years), and
  • still smoke or quit within the past 15 years.

If this sounds like you, ask us whether screening is right for you.

How is lung cancer diagnosed?

Your doctor may use one or more of these tests:

  • Imaging: a chest X-ray, CT scan, PET scan or MRI to look for tumors and see whether cancer has spread.
  • Sputum test: mucus you cough up is checked for cancer cells.
  • Biopsy: a small piece of tissue is removed and checked under a microscope. This is the only way to know for sure. It can be done during a bronchoscopy, with a thin needle passed through the skin using a CT scan as a guide, or with a small surgical procedure.

Once cancer is found, more tests show how far it has spread. This is called staging, and it helps your doctor plan treatment.

How is lung cancer treated?

  • Surgery to remove the cancer is often the best option for early non-small cell lung cancer. Depending on the tumor, the surgeon may remove a small wedge of lung, a whole section (lobe), or rarely an entire lung.
  • Radiation therapy uses high-energy rays to kill cancer cells and shrink tumors.
  • Chemotherapy uses medicines that travel through the body to kill cancer cells, including cells that have spread beyond the lungs.
  • Targeted therapy and immunotherapy are newer medicines. Targeted drugs attack specific changes in the cancer cells; immunotherapy helps your immune system fight the cancer.
  • Clinical trials test new treatments and are open to patients at every stage.

Treatments are often combined. Even when cancer can't be cured, treatment can ease symptoms, improve quality of life and help people live longer.

What is the outlook?

The outlook depends on the type of lung cancer, its stage, the size of the tumor, whether there are symptoms, and your overall health. Finding it early makes the biggest difference.

Can lung cancer be prevented?

  • Don't smoke, or quit. It is never too late, and we can help.
  • Avoid secondhand smoke.
  • Test your home for radon.
  • Take safety precautions if you work around asbestos or other chemicals.
  • Eat plenty of fruits and vegetables.
