Narcolepsy

About 3 min read

Key points

  • Narcolepsy is a long-term brain condition that causes strong sleepiness during the day, no matter how much sleep you get at night.
  • People with narcolepsy can fall asleep suddenly, even while working, talking or driving.
  • It is uncommon, affecting about 1 in 2,000 people, and often starts in the teen or young adult years.
  • There is no cure, but treatment can control symptoms well.

What is narcolepsy?

Normally the brain keeps sleep and wakefulness separate. In narcolepsy, the brain has trouble controlling when you are asleep and awake, so pieces of sleep spill into the day. People feel an overwhelming need to sleep and can drift off at any time. These "sleep attacks" can last from 30 seconds to more than 30 minutes.

Narcolepsy can start at any age, but it most often begins in the teens or early adulthood. Because the sleepiness is often mistaken for laziness, depression or not getting enough sleep, many people go years before being diagnosed.

What are the symptoms?

  • Excessive daytime sleepiness. This is the main symptom and happens to everyone with narcolepsy.
  • Sudden loss of muscle control (cataplexy). Brief weakness, such as a sagging face, drooping head or buckling knees, often set off by strong emotions like laughing or surprise. You stay awake during it. Not everyone has this symptom.
  • Sleep paralysis. Being unable to move or speak for a short time while falling asleep or waking up. It can be frightening, but it passes and is not harmful.
  • Vivid, dream-like images when drifting off to sleep or waking up, which can feel very real.
  • Broken night sleep, waking up many times during the night.

What causes it?

In many people with narcolepsy, the brain has lost cells that make a chemical called hypocretin (also called orexin), which helps keep you awake. Researchers think the immune system may attack these cells by mistake. Genes may also play a part. For some people, the cause isn't known.

How is narcolepsy diagnosed?

  • Your doctor asks about your sleep and your symptoms. You may be asked to keep a sleep diary for a week or two.
  • An overnight sleep study checks your sleep and rules out other problems, such as sleep apnea.
  • The next day, a Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT) measures how quickly you fall asleep during a series of short daytime naps, and whether you go straight into dreaming sleep. Our sleep lab does both tests.

How is narcolepsy treated?

Narcolepsy can't be cured, but most people manage it well with a mix of medicine and daily habits:

  • Medicines to help you stay awake during the day, and others that reduce cataplexy and improve night sleep. Your doctor will find what works best for you.
  • Short, planned naps during the day can boost alertness.
  • A regular sleep schedule, going to bed and waking up at the same times every day.
  • Avoiding alcohol and heavy meals before important activities, since they can make sleepiness worse.

Staying safe

Don't drive or use dangerous machines if you feel sleepy. Talk with your doctor about when it is safe to drive. It can also help to tell your school or workplace, so they can allow for naps or schedule changes.

If you are often very sleepy during the day even after a full night's sleep, talk to us. It may be narcolepsy or another sleep disorder that can be treated.
