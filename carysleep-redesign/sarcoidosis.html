Sarcoidosis

About 3 min read

Key points

  • Sarcoidosis causes tiny clumps of inflamed cells, called granulomas, to form in the body. It most often affects the lungs and the lymph nodes in the chest.
  • No one knows exactly what causes it. It is not contagious.
  • Many people have mild sarcoidosis that gets better on its own. Others need treatment and regular checkups.

What is sarcoidosis?

Sarcoidosis is an inflammatory disease. The body's immune system forms tiny lumps called granulomas. Under a microscope they look like grains of sugar or sand. They can grow and clump together, and if too many form in one organ, that organ may not work as well.

Sarcoidosis usually starts in the lungs or in the lymph nodes in the chest, but it can affect almost any part of the body, including the skin, eyes, joints and heart. In many people it goes away on its own within a few months to a few years; in others it lasts much longer.

What causes sarcoidosis?

Doctors believe it starts when the immune system overreacts to something, such as bacteria, a virus, dust or chemicals, or mistakenly attacks the body's own healthy cells. The exact trigger is unknown. Possible causes being studied include:

  • An infection with a virus or bacteria
  • A problem in the immune system
  • Something in the environment, such as a dust or chemical
  • Genes passed down in families

Who gets sarcoidosis?

It affects tens of thousands of people in the United States. It can happen at any age, but it is most common in young adults between 20 and 40. African Americans and people of Scandinavian, German or Irish descent are more likely to get it. African-American women get it about twice as often as African-American men.

What are the symptoms?

Many people have no symptoms at all, and sarcoidosis is found by chance on a routine chest X-ray. When the lungs are involved, people may have:

  • Shortness of breath
  • A cough that won't go away

Other signs and symptoms

  • Feeling tired or generally unwell
  • Fever and night sweats
  • Trouble sleeping
  • Weight loss or loss of appetite
  • Small red bumps on the face, arms or buttocks
  • Red, tender bumps on the shins, often with aching ankles, wrists or other joints
  • Red, watery or painful eyes, or blurry vision. See an eye doctor if you notice eye changes.

How is sarcoidosis diagnosed?

No single test can prove you have sarcoidosis, and its symptoms look like those of other illnesses. So doctors also rule out conditions such as tuberculosis, lymphoma (cancer of the lymph nodes), rheumatoid arthritis and fungal infections. Tests may include:

  • A chest X-ray, usually first, and sometimes a CT scan
  • Breathing tests and blood tests
  • A biopsy: a small sample of tissue, often taken from the lungs or lymph nodes during a bronchoscopy, to look at under a microscope

How serious is sarcoidosis?

For many people it is mild, and the inflammation gets better on its own. For others it comes and goes, or stays the same without getting worse. In a small number of people it slowly gets worse over the years and leaves scar tissue in the lungs, skin, eyes or other organs, which can affect how they work. Rarely, when vital organs such as the heart are badly affected, sarcoidosis can be life-threatening.

How is sarcoidosis treated?

  • Watching and waiting. If only the lungs and nearby lymph nodes are affected and you feel well, you may not need treatment. Sarcoidosis often clears on its own at this stage. Your doctor will check on you with regular visits and chest X-rays, often for 3 to 12 months.
  • Medicines are usually recommended if the inflammation is getting worse or not improving, or if the heart, eyes or nervous system are involved, or the calcium level in your blood is high. The most common medicine is a steroid called prednisone, which calms the inflammation.
  • Regular follow-up. If you take prednisone long-term, a doctor with experience in sarcoidosis should manage your care and watch for side effects.

Not smoking, avoiding dust and fumes, and keeping up with checkups and eye exams all help protect your health.
