Insomnia

About 3 min read

Key points

  • Insomnia means trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking up too early, so you don't get enough restful sleep.
  • Most adults need 7 to 9 hours of good sleep a night.
  • Insomnia usually has a cause, such as stress, another health problem, a medicine, or sleep habits. Finding the cause is the first step.
  • It is very treatable, often without sleeping pills.

What is insomnia?

Insomnia is not getting enough sleep, or enough restful sleep, to feel and work your best. It can look like:

  • Lying awake for a long time before you fall asleep
  • Waking up often during the night and having trouble getting back to sleep
  • Waking up too early in the morning

How does it affect you during the day?

Symptoms are different for everyone, but may include:

  • Trouble paying attention, concentrating or remembering things
  • Doing poorly at work or school
  • Moodiness or irritability
  • Daytime sleepiness, low energy or low motivation
  • Acting on impulse, or aggression
  • Mistakes or accidents
  • Worry or frustration about your sleep

What causes insomnia?

Insomnia is usually linked to something else. Common causes include:

  • Stress, from everyday work or family pressures to big changes like a death, divorce or job loss.
  • Other sleep disorders. For example, people with restless legs syndrome often have trouble falling asleep, and sleep apnea can wake you many times a night.
  • Medical conditions. Pain, discomfort or limited mobility can make sleep hard. This is more common in older adults. Pregnancy (especially the last three months) and menopause can also disturb sleep.
  • Mental health. Depression and anxiety are common causes of trouble falling or staying asleep. Sometimes insomnia is the first sign of them. Bipolar disorder and other mood disorders can also affect sleep.
  • Medicines and substances. Some cold and allergy medicines (containing pseudoephedrine), antidepressants, and medicines for ADHD, high blood pressure or Parkinson's disease can keep you awake. Caffeine and other stimulants make it hard to fall asleep. Alcohol before bed causes you to wake during the night. Stopping a sleeping pill suddenly can also cause insomnia.
  • Your bedroom. Noise, light, a room that is too hot or cold, or a bed partner who snores can all disrupt sleep.
  • Habits and schedules. Going to bed at different times, or working shifts and sleeping during the day, can throw off your body clock.

Simple habits that help

  • Go to bed and get up at the same time every day, even on weekends.
  • Keep your bedroom dark, quiet and cool, and use your bed only for sleep.
  • Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening, and avoid alcohol close to bedtime.
  • Put screens away for the last hour before bed and do something relaxing.
  • If you can't sleep after about 20 minutes, get up and do something quiet until you feel sleepy.

How is insomnia treated?

We start by looking for the cause, such as another sleep disorder, a medical problem or a medicine. Treating that often improves sleep. Other treatments include:

  • Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I): a short program that changes the thoughts and habits that keep you awake. It is the recommended first treatment for long-term insomnia and works well for most people.
  • Medicines for short-term use in some cases, chosen carefully with your doctor.
  • A sleep study if your doctor suspects another sleep disorder, such as sleep apnea.

If poor sleep has lasted more than a few weeks or is affecting your days, talk to us. Our clinic offers sleep evaluations and an insomnia handout on the Patient Forms page.
